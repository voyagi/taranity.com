We will declare our bias up front: we run a boutique studio, so of course we think there is
a case for one. But we have also worked alongside big agencies and next to plenty of
freelancers, and the honest truth is that none of the three is best. They are best at
different things, and most of the frustration people have with whoever they hired comes
from picking the wrong shape for the job, not from the people being bad at it.

So if you are weighing a boutique studio against an agency and a freelancer to build your
website, app, or software, here is the real trade-off, without the part where everyone
claims to be perfect for everyone.

## The freelancer: cheapest, fastest, thinnest

A good freelancer is a fantastic deal for a well-defined, contained piece of work. You want
a landing page built, a specific feature added, a design done: a freelancer is often the
right call. Direct, affordable, no overhead, and the best ones are genuinely excellent.

Where it gets risky is anything bigger than one person can hold in their head, or anything
that needs to outlive their availability:

- **The bus factor is one.** They get sick, take a better contract, or simply go quiet, and
  your project stops. There is no one else who knows how it works.
- **One person rarely spans the whole stack well.** A great designer is rarely also a great
  back-end engineer who is also good at infrastructure and also thinks about security. You
  end up either stitching together several freelancers yourself (now you are the project
  manager) or accepting gaps.
- **Continuity is fragile.** Six months later you need a change and they have moved on, and
  whoever you hire next has to reverse-engineer undocumented work.

Best for: defined, bounded projects where you can absorb the risk of it being one person.

## The big agency: capacity, process, and a price for it

Large agencies solve the freelancer's fragility with scale. There is a team, a process, an
account manager, redundancy if someone leaves, and the capacity to take on something large.
For a big company with a big budget and a need for that machinery, this is the right answer.

The costs are real, though, and they are not only financial:

- **You often do not get the people you met.** The senior team that won the pitch and the
  team that actually does your work are frequently not the same people. The A-team sells,
  and the work flows down to whoever is available.
- **The overhead is in the price.** Account managers, sales, office space, layers of
  coordination: you pay for all of it, whether or not it adds anything to your project.
- **You can be a small fish.** If your budget is modest by their standards, you get modest
  attention and you feel it. Your urgent is their routine.
- **Handoffs everywhere.** Strategy hands to design hands to development hands to QA, and
  things get lost in the gaps between teams that do not sit together.

Best for: large, complex programmes where you need capacity and process more than intimacy,
and the budget supports it.

## The boutique studio: senior people, fewer of them

This is the middle option people forget exists, and it is the one we will obviously defend,
so take it with the appropriate salt. A small studio is a handful of senior people who do
the work themselves. No A-team bait-and-switch, because the people you meet are the people
who build it. Less overhead than an agency, more breadth and continuity than a freelancer.

What you are actually buying is seniority without the agency tax. The people are
experienced enough to span design and engineering, small enough that you are a real client
and not a ticket, and stable enough that the same people are there when you need a change
next year. We describe it as small by choice, senior by default: not because small is
trendy, but because it is the structure that lets senior people stay close to the actual
work instead of managing other people doing it.

The honest limits:

- **A studio cannot scale to a 50-person programme.** If you genuinely need that much
  capacity at once, an agency fits better. A studio is deliberately not built for that.
- **Availability is finite.** Good small studios are often booked, because the whole point
  is that they do not take on more than they can do well. You may have to wait for a slot.
- **You are trusting a small team's judgement.** That is a strength when they are good and a
  risk if they are not, so the vetting matters more. Look at what they have actually
  shipped, not what they say.

Best for: serious projects where craft, senior attention, and continuity matter more than
raw headcount, which covers most websites, commerce builds, and products for small to
mid-sized companies.

## How to actually decide

Strip away the labels and ask three questions about your project:

1. **How big and how long-lived is it?** A bounded one-off leans freelancer. A large,
   multi-team programme leans agency. Most everything in between, the thing you will live
   with and keep evolving, leans studio.
2. **How much do you need design and engineering to be one conversation?** If the seams
   between how it looks and how it works matter to you (they usually do), you want the
   people doing both in the same room. Studios and full-stack freelancers do this
   naturally. Agencies often do not.
3. **What is the cost of it breaking after launch?** The higher that cost, the more you
   should weight continuity, meaning who is still here in a year to fix and extend it. That
   pushes away from the lone freelancer and toward a small team you can keep.

None of this requires you to hire a studio, and we would rather you pick the right shape
than the flattering one. But if your project is the in-between kind, too important and
long-lived for a single freelancer, not big enough to need an agency's machinery, and you
care about the result being good and not just done, that gap is exactly what a boutique
studio is for.
