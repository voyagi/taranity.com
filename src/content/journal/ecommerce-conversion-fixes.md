When sales dip, the first instinct is almost always "we need a redesign". It is also the
slowest and most expensive way to react, and usually the wrong one. A redesign takes three months,
costs a fortune, resets everything you have learned about your current store, and (the part
nobody tells you) frequently lowers conversions for the first few weeks while the new
layout settles and you re-find what worked.

We would rather you keep the money. The truth is that most stores bleed sales in a few
specific, unglamorous places, and you can seal them one at a time. Here is where we look
first when a brand asks us to lift its conversion rate, in the order we would actually do
the work.

## 1. Fix the checkout before anything else

The Baymard Institute has tracked online cart abandonment for years and the number sits
around 70 percent. Seven of ten people who add to cart leave without buying. You will never
get that to zero, but a meaningful chunk of it is self-inflicted, and the checkout is where
the self-inflicted damage concentrates:

- **Forced account creation.** The single most cited reason people abandon a cart. Add a
  guest checkout. You can ask them to create an account after the order, when they are
  happy, not as a toll gate before payment.
- **Surprise costs at the last step.** Shipping, tax, and fees that only appear on the
  final screen. Show them early. People do not abandon because shipping costs money. They
  abandon because it appeared after they had mentally committed.
- **Too many fields.** Every field is a chance to quit. Cut anything you do not truly need
  to fulfil the order, and use address autocomplete instead of seven manual fields.

If you only do one thing on this list, do this one. Checkout is where the money is closest
to your hand and slips out anyway.

## 2. Make your product images do the selling

People cannot touch your product, so the images are the product. One catalogue shot on a
white background is not enough. The stores that convert show scale (the thing in a hand, on
a desk, on a person), show texture up close, and show it in use. If you sell anything where
fit or size matters, a short video or a 360 view earns its keep fast. This is not art
direction for its own sake. It removes the doubt that stops the click.

## 3. Put the reason to trust where the doubt is

Reviews, ratings, return policy, security badges, real photos from real customers. The
trick is placement. Trust signals belong next to the moment of hesitation, not buried on a
separate page. The return policy matters most right beside the Add to Cart button, because
that is the exact second the visitor is thinking "what if it is wrong". Answer the worry
where it happens.

## 4. Speed, yes, again

We will not relitigate the whole case here (there is a
[separate piece on website speed and conversions](/journal/website-speed-conversions)), but
for a store the stakes are higher, because you have more pages, more images, and more
scripts than a brochure site. Every second on a product page is paid for in lost carts.
Measure your load time on a real phone before you blame the funnel.

## 5. Write product copy that answers the question, not the brief

Most product descriptions are written for the brand and read like a press release.
Customers are asking specific questions. Will it fit my space? Is it the heavy version or
the light one? What is it made of? How long does it last? Good copy answers those in plain
words, in the order people ask them. "Premium, thoughtfully crafted" sells nothing. "38 cm
wide, fits a standard shelf, holds about 6 kg" sells.

## 6. Stop hiding the price and the shipping time

Confidence converts. A clear price, a clear "in stock", and a clear "arrives in 2 to 3
days" beat a vague, hopeful page every time. If your shipping is slow, saying so honestly
costs you fewer sales than a surprise at checkout does, and it costs you far fewer refunds
and angry emails after.

## 7. Then, and only then, test changes one at a time

Once the obvious leaks are sealed, now you A/B test: button copy, product page layout, the
order of trust signals. The reason this comes last is that testing a broken checkout is
like tuning the radio in a car with a flat tyre. Fix the structural stuff first, measure,
then optimise at the margins.

## What this adds up to

None of these is a redesign. Together they often recover more revenue than a redesign
would, at a fraction of the cost and risk, and they leave you with data instead of a fresh
coat of paint. The brands that win at this treat the store as something you tune
continuously, not something you rip out every two years. It is most of what
[building commerce](/#crafts) actually looks like once the launch is behind you.
