Automation has a marketing problem. It is sold as a magic wand ("automate your entire
business"), which sets you up to either do nothing because it is overwhelming, or automate
the wrong thing first and conclude it does not work. Both are common. Neither is necessary.

After building automation for a lot of small companies, we have landed on a boring but
reliable way to decide business process automation for small business. Do not start with
the tool, and do not start with the most painful task. Start with a list and a simple
filter.

## The filter: frequent, rule-based, and boring

A task is a good first candidate to automate when it is all three of these:

1. **Frequent.** It happens daily or many times a week. Automating something that happens
   twice a year is a hobby, not a return.
2. **Rule-based.** The steps are the same every time, and a human follows a procedure
   rather than making a real judgement call. "If invoice paid, send receipt and update the
   sheet" automates. "Decide whether this client is worth keeping" does not.
3. **Boring.** Nobody enjoys it, mistakes are easy, and the work adds no judgement or
   relationship value. These are the tasks where a human's attention is wasted and
   error-prone at the same time.

The sweet spot is the overlap of all three. Resist the urge to automate the hard,
interesting, judgement-heavy work first. That is where automation fails loudly and where a
human is genuinely adding value.

## Where the overlap usually lives

In most small businesses, the first wins are in the same few places:

- **The copy-paste between tools.** Someone takes data out of one system and types it into
  another: orders into the accounting tool, leads from a form into the CRM, hours into
  payroll. This is the purest automation candidate that exists. It is frequent, it is
  identical every time, and it is exactly where typos and dropped records happen.
- **Follow-ups and reminders.** Payment reminders, appointment confirmations, "your order
  shipped", review requests, the third nudge to a lead who went quiet. Reliable, scheduled
  communication that today depends on someone remembering.
- **Reporting that gets assembled by hand.** The weekly numbers someone pulls from four
  places into a spreadsheet every Monday morning. Automating the assembly (not the
  thinking) frees the most senior, most expensive time in the building.
- **Onboarding checklists.** New customer or new hire, same sequence of accounts created,
  emails sent, documents requested. Easy to forget a step manually, trivial to make
  consistent.

## The order we would actually go in

Pick the task that scores highest on "frequent, rule-based, and boring" and lowest on "what
happens if it goes wrong". That second part matters. Your first automation should be one
where a glitch is annoying, not catastrophic. Automating refund payments on day one is how
you learn caution the expensive way. Automating "move the lead into the CRM" is how you
build trust in the system before you hand it anything that touches money.

So start with an internal copy-paste task, prove it works for two weeks, then move toward
the customer-facing and money-touching stuff with the confidence you earned.

## The traps that waste the money

A few honest warnings, because automation done badly is worse than no automation:

**Automating a broken process.** If the manual process is a mess, automating it just makes
the mess happen faster and more reliably. Fix the process on paper first, then automate the
clean version. This is the most common and most expensive mistake.

**The tool graveyard.** It is easy to sign up for five automation tools that each do one
thing and never talk to each other, and now you are paying monthly for a system more
fragile than the manual work it replaced. Fewer tools, better connected, beats a drawer
full of half-used subscriptions.

**No human in the loop where one is needed.** Full automation is the goal for the boring
stuff, but plenty of tasks want a human approval step in the middle: the system does the
work, a person clicks yes before it sends. That hybrid is underrated, and it prevents the
nightmare where an automation cheerfully emails 4,000 wrong invoices overnight.

**Forgetting it exists.** An automation needs an owner and an occasional check. APIs
change, a tool updates, and a silent failure means receipts stopped going out three weeks
ago and nobody noticed. Build in a "tell me if this breaks" alert from day one.

## The realistic payoff

Done right, the first few automations in a small business usually give back a few hours a
week of someone's time and cut a category of small errors to near zero. That is not
revolutionary on paper. In practice it is the difference between the owner doing
administrivia at 9pm and the owner doing the work only they can do. The point of automating
the boring is to buy back attention for the parts of the business that actually need a
human.

Start with one frequent, rule-based, low-risk task. Make it boringly reliable. Then do the
